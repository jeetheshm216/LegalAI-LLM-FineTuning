A private contractual clause purporting to waive a Fundamental Right, such as agreeing not to challenge an unconstitutional State action, would ordinarily not be enforceable, though the exact effect can depend on which right and which facts are involved, so specialist advice may be needed for a specific clause.

**Model Answer:**

Once a summons is served, the accused must appear before the court on the specified date. Failing to do so can result in the court issuing a warrant for the accused's arrest, and if the accused remains untraced, the court may proceed with the trial in their absence under Section 317 BNSS. In practice, if the accused believes the case is false, the correct course is to file a written reply challenging the charges and seeking adjournments to prepare a defence, rather than ignoring the summons.

**Reference Answer:**
No. A person who has been validly summoned by a court must appear or otherwise respond through the legal process (such as through a lawyer, or by applying for exemption from personal appearance where permitted); simply ignoring a summons can lead to further coercive process, such as a bailable or non-bailable warrant, or proceedings for non-appearance. If the person believes the case is false or an abuse of process, the correct remedy is to defend the case through the court process, including remedies like a petition for quashing where genuinely available -- not to ignore the summons.

**Key Points:**
- Ignoring a summons risks a warrant or coercive process
- Belief that the case is false does not excuse non-appearance
- Correct route is to respond through the legal process (appear, engage counsel, seek permitted exemptions)

## [eval_036] Criminal Law (Difficulty: medium)

**Question:**
What is 'discharge' in a criminal case, and how is it different from acquittal?

**Model Answer:**
'Discharge' under Section 207 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) is a procedural order that can be passed at any stage of a trial, including after framing of charges but before conviction, directing the accused to be released from custody. It is distinct from an acquittal, which is a final determination of innocence on merits. Discharge is typically granted when the court finds there is no sufficient evidence against the accused, or when the offence is compoundable and the parties have settled their differences.

**Reference Answer:**
Discharge occurs at a preliminary stage, before the trial properly begins, when the court examines the material on record and concludes there is no sufficient ground to proceed against the accused, so the accused is discharged without a full trial. Acquittal, by contrast, happens at the conclusion of a trial (after evidence has been led and arguments heard), where the court finds that the prosecution has not proved the charge beyond reasonable doubt. Discharge is a pre-trial screening mechanism, while acquittal is a post-trial finding on the merits.

**Key Points:**
- Discharge: pre-trial finding of insufficient material to proceed
- Acquittal: post-trial finding that guilt is not proved beyond reasonable doubt
- Discharge happens before evidence is led at trial; acquittal happens after
- Both result in the accused not being convicted, but at different stages

Can a tenant who has occupied a rented property for many years automatically become the owner through adverse possession?

**Model Answer:**
No. The Supreme Court's ruling in Vineeta Sharma v. In practice, this means a tenant cannot claim ownership simply by staying in a property long enough — the landlord retains title, but may lose the right to physically evict if the limitation period passes without action.

**Reference Answer:**

A tenant would first need to clearly and openly repudiate the landlord's title and assert hostile possession, and even then the clock for adverse possession would generally only start running from that repudiation, which is a fact-specific and difficult claim to establish.

**Key Points:**
- Permissive possession (like a tenancy) cannot mature into adverse possession merely by lapse of time
- Adverse possession requires possession hostile to the true owner, open and continuous
- A tenant would need to clearly repudiate the landlord's title first
- Such claims are fact-intensive and hard to establish
